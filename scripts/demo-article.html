Designing for Long-Form Reading

Notes on the parts of a document that only matter once it gets long enough to get lost in.

Why structure beats search

Search answers a question you already know how to ask. Structure answers the one you cannot phrase yet: where am I, and what else is here? A reader three screens into an unfamiliar specification has the second question, not the first.

The cost of scrolling blind

Every scroll without a landmark is a small act of faith. The reader is spending attention on navigation that should have gone to the argument.

Multiply that by a forty-screen specification and the reader is no longer reading; they are administering the page.

What a good outline promises

Three things: the shape of the whole, the location of the part, and a way to move between them without losing either.

It has to keep all three true while the reader moves, which is why an outline that is merely printed once at the top stops helping on the second screen.

Headings are an interface

Authors write headings for the page. Readers use them as controls. The gap between those two intentions is where most long documents fail.

Treat the heading list as a control surface and the failures become obvious: duplicates, headings that are really labels, and levels that encode visual weight rather than meaning.

Hierarchy that survives skimming

Two levels are usually enough to hold a document in the head. A third is useful for reference material and actively harmful for prose.

Depth should be the reader's choice, not the document's, because the same page is a reference for one person and a narrative for another.

Naming sections honestly

A heading that reads well in sequence and badly out of context is a heading that will not work in an outline.

Write them so they survive being lifted out and stacked in a list, which is exactly what any outline will do to them.

Position, continuously

Knowing which section you are in is not the same as knowing how far through it you are. Both are cheap to show and expensive to guess.

Position has to update continuously, not on click, or it becomes a record of where the reader last interacted rather than where they are.

Progress as a second signal

A count and a bar say different things. One is discrete and comparable; the other is continuous and glanceable.

Together they answer “how much is left” and “how many of these are there” without the reader doing arithmetic.

Getting out of the way

The best state for a reading aid is dormant. It should be reachable in one gesture and invisible the rest of the time.

Anything that occupies attention while not in use is competing with the text it exists to serve.

Where controls should live

Near the thumb on a phone, near the margin on a desk. Neither is a scaled version of the other.

A phone wants a control that can be moved out of the way with one hand; a desk wants one that can sit open in the margin all session.

Remembering the reader's choice

If someone moves a control, that is a preference, not an accident. Put it back where they left it, per site.

Per site, not globally: the right corner on a documentation site is the wrong corner on a news site with a sticky header.

What this cannot fix

A page with no headings has no structure to show. Styling text to look like a heading does not create one, and no amount of heuristics turns presentation back into meaning.

Reader view and native PDF rendering are closed to extensions entirely, so those pages are out of reach no matter how good the heuristics get.
